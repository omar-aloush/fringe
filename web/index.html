Executive Summary (General Audience)

During the 2012–2016 drought, farms in California's Central Valley pumped so much groundwater that the valley floor visibly sank—up to about 5 cm in a single month near Corcoran. The clay layers under the valley collapsed like a squeezed sponge, and that collapse is permanent: the aquifer will never hold quite as much water as before.

1. What We Observed

Satellite radar and NASA/JPL analyses show the San Joaquin Valley sinking up to ~5 cm per month at the drought peak, with rates above 300 mm/yr near Corcoran. Continuous GNSS stations P307 and P304 independently verified 38 cm and 21 cm of true ground drop.

2. The Physical Mechanism (Aquifer Collapse)

Drought cut surface-water deliveries. When water pressure inside the interbedded clay layers drops, pore space collapses under overburden pressure. The clays compress permanently: future rain refilling the aquifer cannot restore the destroyed volume.

3. Why It Matters

The Central Valley produces a quarter of America's food. Canals, aqueducts, and bridges were engineered for stable ground. Sinking buckles concrete canals, reduces flow gradient, and permanently deprives California of strategic water buffers.
